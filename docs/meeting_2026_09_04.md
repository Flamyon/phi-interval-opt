## 0. Orientation

The framework of [1] was read from the paper itself, claim by claim, and every
coefficient, hypothesis and numbered statement this project uses was recorded
with its example number and printed page before any of it was implemented. The
problems were then formulated so that the three orders can actually differ:
the literal reading of slide 19, a constant half-width ±ε, makes all three
coincide with each other and with the crisp order, which was measured before it
was believed, so the half-width was made a function of the decision vector
instead. For the two-variable problem p1 the φ-efficient sets were then derived
in closed form under all three φ, by instantiating your Theorem 3.3, Example 3.8
and Example 3.9 on that problem and inventing nothing: the stationarity condition
(15) becomes a diagonal linear system, the solution map w ↦ x(w) is an explicit
rational function of the weight vector, and eliminating the weights gives three
explicit regions of the decision plane. **That derivation already answers, for
p1 and exactly, the question this study exists to ask**: changing the order from
Example 2.2 to Example 2.4 on the same problem, 60.5% of what was optimal stops
being optimal and 87.7% of the new optimal set was not optimal before, the two
sets sharing 10.3% of their union; §4.3. Three solvers were built — random search
as the *referencia base*, NSGA-II and multiobjective PSO, both from `pymoo` and
unmodified — and validated against those derived sets over ninety measurements.
One of the gate's two assertions passes in ninety of ninety: no solver ever
returns a point that beats the derivation. The other fails in twelve of ninety,
all of them NSGA-II, and that failure is understood, bounded and reported as a
finding rather than repaired. The experiments themselves are the
last three steps.

**What has not been done.** No experiment has been run, so there is no
*experimental* result about φ: the sentence above is analytic, it holds for one
two-variable problem, and whether that magnitude survives at thirty and twelve
variables is exactly what the benchmark runs are for. Nothing has been measured
on ZDT1 and DTLZ2 beyond six diagnostic configurations. The portfolio application, which is
where "which φ gives better results" becomes a well-posed question, has not been
started. And the derivation of the efficient sets exists for p1 only: p0 turned
out not to serve as a fixture, and for the benchmarks no closed form exists.

### 2.1 The class of admissible automorphisms

[1] section 2, unnumbered display, printed page 3, lines 163–171, denotes by 𝔄_m
the class of automorphisms φ = φ_1 × ⋯ × φ_m of ℝ^{2m} acting componentwise on
endpoint pairs,

```
φ_i(x_{2i−1}, x_{2i}) = (λ_{2i−1} x_{2i−1} + λ_{2i} x_{2i},
                         β_{2i−1} x_{2i−1} + β_{2i} x_{2i}),
```

for all i ∈ {1, …, m}. The admissibility condition is stated in the same
sentence, printed page 3, lines 171–172, and restated in section 3, printed page
6, lines 398–399:

> using real numbers (that can be chosen according to a decision) λ_{2i−1},
> λ_{2i}, β_{2i−1}, β_{2i} ∈ ℝ with λ_{2i−1} β_{2i} ≠ λ_{2i} β_{2i−1}.

**That is a non-vanishing determinant and nothing more.** Sections 2 and 3 were
read in full and no further restriction on the coefficients appears anywhere in
them: no normalisation, no sign condition, no orientation condition. Two
consequences the project relies on: any coefficient pair with non-zero
determinant is admissible by your own definition, so a one-parameter family

### 2.2 The order, and the codomain

Definition 2.1, printed page 2, lines 123–134, defines the preference order
through an injective map φ̄ from (𝒞)^m into ℝ^{2m},

> A ≦^{2m}_φ B ⇔ φ̄(A) ⩽_{ℝ^{2m}} φ̄(B),   (1)

for an arbitrary bijection φ and an arbitrary order ⩽_{ℝ^{2m}}. **Componentwise-
ness is not part of Definition 2.1**; it arrives when the paper specialises to
𝔄_m and fixes ⩽_{ℝ^{2m}} to be ≦^{2m}, at lines 172–176. Equation (2), printed
page 3, is that componentwise order, and equation (3), printed page 3, lines
179–184, is the decomposition the project depends on:

> (A_1, …, A_m) ≦_φ (B_1, …, B_m) ⇔ φ̄_i(A_i) ≦ φ̄_i(B_i) ∀ i ∈ {1, …, m}.

So φ is applied independently to each interval objective. The strict versions are
equations (6) and (7), printed page 3, and ≦_φ is a partial order by Propositions
2.1 and 2.2.

**m interval objectives become 2m real objectives.** Equation (12) at printed
page 8 line 511 fixes the target form, the composition display at lines 517–532
writes φ̄ ∘ F out as (Λ_1^T f, B_1^T f, …, Λ_m^T f, B_m^T f) with

> Λ_i^T f = λ_{2i−1} f_i + λ_{2i} f̄_i,   B_i^T f = β_{2i−1} f_i + β_{2i} f̄_i,

and equation (13) at line 537 is (MOP_φ), min_{x∈S} (φ̄∘F)(x). This is the
structural fact everything downstream rests on: ZDT1 with m = 2 becomes a

## 3. Formulating the problems

This is the section where the mathematics decides whether the study measures
anything at all.

### 3.1 The degeneracy: why a constant width makes all three orders coincide

Write each interval objective in centre and half-width form,
F_i(x) = [c_i(x) − r_i(x), c_i(x) + r_i(x)]. In those coordinates the three φ read

> φ_LU : (c − r, c + r),   φ_LS : (c − r, 2r),   φ_CW : (c, r).

**The geometric argument.** Suppose r_i is constant on S, or more generally that
r_i = ψ(c_i) for some function ψ. Then the image of S in the (c_i, r_i) plane is
a **curve**, not a two-dimensional region. Each φ_i is an injective linear map of
that plane, so it takes a monotone curve to a monotone curve, and along a
monotone curve the componentwise order of ℝ^2 is a total order that does not
depend on which injective monotone reparametrisation was used. Every φ therefore
induces the same order on the image, and it is the order induced by c_i alone,
which is the crisp order. The study would be measuring nothing — and measuring
nothing by construction, not by result.

**The measurement that confirms it.** With F_i(x) = [f_i(x) − ε, f_i(x) + ε],
which is the literal reading of slide 19, on ZDT1 (n = 30, m = 2) and DTLZ2
(n = 12, m = 3) at ε ∈ {0.05, 0.5}, over 5000 uniform points of the box, in
exact arithmetic: the crisp non-dominated set and the three φ non-dominated sets
are **identical as index sets**, not merely equal in size — 16, 24, 147 and 152
points in the four cases. `docs/a1_uncertainty_model.md` part 1.

And the second clause is not implied by the first. A width far from constant but
an exact function of the centre does the same: proportional imprecision
f ↦ [f(1−ε), f(1+ε)] on ZDT1 at ε ∈ {0.10, 0.25} gives width spans of 0.84 and
2.19, correlation +1.0000, and again all four index sets identical. That
construction is kept as a negative control.

There is a trap here that is worth stating, because it is the reason this project
insists on exact arithmetic in this one place. The *expected* symptom of a
degenerate model is that the three φ coincide, which is easy to notice. The
*floating-point* symptom is that they differ: computing the width as f̄ − f in
double precision on a construction whose true width is constant leaves
cancellation noise of order ε_mach·|f| as the entire content of the second image
coordinate of φ_LS and φ_CW, and on ZDT1 at ε = 0.05 that gives |crisp| = |φ_LU|
= 16 against |φ_LS| = |φ_CW| = 30. A study that ran the degenerate construction
in double precision would have reported "φ_LS and φ_CW recover a larger efficient
set" and would have been reporting arithmetic. Section 7.4 is what was done about
it.

### 3.2 The condition this imposes, and the model chosen

The condition is: **the half-width must vary independently of the centre as x
moves through the decision space**, so that the image of S in the (c, r) plane is
two-dimensional. It is necessary and it is not sufficient; section 3.4 gives a
construction that satisfies it on a uniform sample and is still degenerate where
it matters.

Slide 19 offers two constructions for the small problems, imprecision in the
coefficients or in the objective. **The objective was chosen, and the reason is
structural rather than a preference.** If a coefficient a becomes an interval
[a−d, a+d] and the objective is a monomial in a — f = a·x, say — then the centre
is a·x and the half-width is d·|x|, so the half-width is an **exact linear
function of the centre**. That is the degeneracy of 3.1 in its second form. It is
worst precisely on slide 19's "problemas sencillos de una o dos variables", which
is where objectives are monomials. Measured on ZDT1 with f_1 = c_1 x_1 and
c_1 ∈ [1−d, 1+d]: correlation +1.0000 and φ_CW returning the crisp non-dominated
set exactly, at d = 0.10 and at d = 0.25.

So the construction, fixed once for the whole project, is

> F_i(x) = [ f_i(x) − r_i(x), f_i(x) + r_i(x) ],

with f_i the objective of the underlying problem, untouched, and r_i > 0 a
function of the decision vector. It is slide 19's own form — "incertidumbre
acotada ±ε en los objetivos" — with the single change that ε becomes a function
of x. Two further properties recommend it. The crisp problem is recovered exactly
at r ≡ 0, so the sweep is a sweep in one parameter of one problem; under
coefficient imprecision the collapse changes the objective values themselves and
not only their width. And in the (c, r) form the second image coordinate of φ_LS
and φ_CW at the crisp baseline is exactly zero rather than a cancellation
residue.

The crisp case is nonetheless a **labelled degenerate baseline** and not a data
point in the sensitivity study: at ε = 0 half of the transformed objectives are
identically zero and all three φ coincide with the crisp order, which is 3.1
again.

identically zero. Applying Definition 3.1 directly gives the optimal set as the
whole box under φ_LU and φ_LS and the single point {0} under φ_CW. A reference
front that is the image of the whole box measures nothing. p0 is therefore a
smoke test — does a solver find x = 0 — and p1 is the fixture.

**p1 is the project's own design**, and every constant in it is forced by a
stated requirement.

```
box       x = (x_1, x_2) ∈ [−1/2, 3/2]²
centres   c_1 = x_1² + (x_2 − 1)²,     c_2 = (x_1 − 1)² + (x_2 − 1)²
widths    r_1 = ρ x_2² + δ,            r_2 = ρ x_1² + δ
objectives F_i(x) = [c_i − r_i, c_i + r_i],  i = 1, 2
parameters ρ = 1/4, δ = 1/8
```

*Why the widths are quadratic and non-monotone.* Four earlier designs were built
and measured and all four failed, each for a reason that constrains the next.
If the two half-widths are **opposed** in the same width variable, one increasing
and one decreasing, the width columns alone make every pair of points with
different x_2 incomparable and φ_LS and φ_CW return the whole box. If they are
**aligned** while the centres do not respond to the width variable, φ_LU and φ_LS
return the whole box. If the centres respond to the width variable
**monotonically**, φ_LU and φ_LS coincide exactly. What survives all three is the
structure above: the centre's optimum and the half-width's optimum in the width
variable must both be interior and different, which forces the half-width to be
non-monotone, hence convex quadratic, with curvature small enough to keep c − r
convex.

*Why the widths are driven by different variables.* The first version of p1 had
r_1 = r_2, which makes two of the four transformed columns the same function of
x under φ_LS and φ_CW, so a nominally four-objective transformed problem is
effectively three-objective. Driving r_1 by x_2 and r_2 by x_1 removes that. The
change is not free: the φ_LU and φ_LS efficient sets stop being products of
intervals, and section 4 derives regions with curved boundaries for those two.

*Why ρ = 1/4, and why the box is not the unit box.* Condition (15) is
unconstrained stationarity and carries no constraint multipliers, unlike Examples
3.4 to 3.7, so the project reads Example 3.9 as an interior condition and applies
it only at interior points. The stationary point of c − r in its width variable
is at 1/(1−ρ): at ρ = 1/2 that is 2, outside any reasonable box, and a grid check
at ρ = 1/2 returned a φ_LU efficient set running to a face. At ρ = 1/4 it is 4/3,
and [−1/2, 3/2]² is the smallest symmetric box leaving a margin around it.
Section 4.3 confirms that every derived set is strictly interior, with a margin
of at least 1/6 on every side. δ > 0 keeps the half-width strictly positive, over
[0.1250, 0.6875] on the box, so no interval degenerates; δ = 1/8 rather than 1/10
because δ enters every image coordinate as an **additive constant**, verified in
exact integer arithmetic, so it moves no gradient, no Hessian and no efficient
set, and the dyadic value makes the arithmetic exact on a dyadic sample.

*Why the efficient set is a band and not a curve.* This was a stated requirement
of the design and it cannot be met. Two variables and two interval objectives
give, through Theorem 3.1, a real multiobjective problem with **four** objectives
on a two-dimensional decision space, and for k objectives on an n-dimensional
space the efficient set is generically min(k−1, n)-dimensional, here min(3, 2) =
2. A curve would require the width coordinate and the centre coordinate to have
the same optimum in the width variable — which is exactly the aligned case shown
above to make φ_CW reproduce the crisp order. **A curve and three distinct φ are
not simultaneously available at n = 2, m = 2.** The band was accepted; it gives
coverage and spread two dimensions of structure rather than one, and section 5
is where that turned out to be the harder test. Row s-08.

### 3.4 The benchmarks, and why they needed different width forms

Both keep their published objectives untouched and gain a half-width. The width
driver is the last decision variable in both cases. The **form** differs, and
that is forced by the benchmarks' own g functions rather than being an
inconsistency.

**ZDT1**, n = 30, m = 2, from [2] definition 4, equation (7), printed page 177:
f_1 = x_1, g = 1 + 9·(Σ_{i≥2} x_i)/(n−1), f_2 = g(1 − √(f_1/g)). Here g is
**linear** in x_n and its optimum in x_n is at the face x_n = 0. A half-width
linear in x_n is therefore either aligned with it or opposed to it, and both were
measured and both fail. This is the finding of phase a with the most consequence,
because it is a finding about the *diagnostic* and not about a problem: with
r = ε x_n the construction passes every width-versus-centre statistic on a
uniform sample of the box — width span 0.4999, correlation −0.0111 and +0.1027,
within-centre-bin width spread above 0.99 of the span, and the three φ separate,
|φ_CW| = 86 against |crisp| = 24. Restricted to the slice where ZDT1's efficient
set actually lives, x_2 … x_{29} held at 0 and (x_1, x_n) on an 81 × 81 grid at
ε = 0.25:

| set | \|eff\| | fraction | x_n extent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| crisp | 81 | 0.0123 | [0, 0] |
| φ_LU | 6561 | 1.0000 | [0, 1] |
| φ_LS | 6561 | 1.0000 | [0, 1] |
| φ_CW | 81 | 0.0123 | [0, 0] — identical to crisp |

The uniform sample reported separation where the efficient sets coincide, because
a uniform sample of a 30-dimensional box contains essentially nothing near the
efficient set. Reversing the alignment breaks it the other way: g is linear in
x_n and so is the width, the trade-off has no interior resolution, and every φ
returns the whole slice. So ZDT1's half-width is **quadratic**,
r = ε((x_n − 1/2)² + 1/20), whose optimum is interior at x_n = 1/2 and therefore
different from g's, with the additive 1/20 keeping it strictly positive there.

**DTLZ2**, n = 12, m = 3, from [3] section VII.B, equation (9): g = Σ_{i≥3}
(x_i − 1/2)², which is already **quadratic** in x_n with an interior optimum at
x_n = 1/2. A half-width **linear** in x_n, r = ε x_n, has its optimum at 0 and
therefore already differs from g's, and the trade-off is bounded. No extra
curvature is needed and none should be added: a half-width of the form
(x_n − 1/2)² would put the width's optimum exactly on g's and reintroduce the
alignment.

The levels swept are ε ∈ {0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50}. At every positive level, on
both benchmarks and on the slice, all three φ are distinct and φ_CW differs from
the crisp order. The check on the slice was built as an assertion in both problem
modules rather than assumed; whether the slice check is the right strengthening
is row s-07. The residual is that this width-driver rule was derived from two
benchmarks and one tier 0 design and is a **working rule and not a result**: it
is re-run in full on every new problem, including the portfolio returns of part 2.

## 4. The ground truth: the φ-efficient sets of p1 in closed form

This is the section you are best placed to check, and the one most likely to end
up in the memoria. Everything in it is an instantiation of published results of
[1] on p1; no condition is invented, extended or weakened anywhere.
`docs/b1_phi_efficient_sets.md` is the full derivation. The symbolic work was
done in exact rational arithmetic.

**The figure to put on screen first is `results/meeting/derived_regions_p1.png`**,
which draws the three sets of section 4.3 on the decision plane. It shows the

### 4.1 The image coordinates, and φ-convexity by Theorem 3.3

Writing the four image coordinates g_1, …, g_4 of the transformed problem in the
order (Λ_1^T f, B_1^T f, Λ_2^T f, B_2^T f):

**φ_LU** (Example 2.2)

```
g_1 = c_1 − r_1 = x_1² + (3/4) x_2² − 2 x_2 + 7/8
g_2 = c_1 + r_1 = x_1² + (5/4) x_2² − 2 x_2 + 9/8
g_3 = c_2 − r_2 = (3/4) x_1² − 2 x_1 + x_2² − 2 x_2 + 15/8
g_4 = c_2 + r_2 = (5/4) x_1² − 2 x_1 + x_2² − 2 x_2 + 17/8
```

**φ_LS** (Example 2.3)

```
g_1 = c_1 − r_1,  g_2 = 2 r_1 = (1/2) x_2² + 1/4,
g_3 = c_2 − r_2,  g_4 = 2 r_2 = (1/2) x_1² + 1/4
```

**φ_CW** (Example 2.4)

```
g_1 = c_1 = x_1² + (x_2 − 1)²,   g_2 = r_1 = (1/4) x_2² + 1/8,
g_3 = c_2 = (x_1 − 1)² + (x_2 − 1)²,  g_4 = r_2 = (1/4) x_1² + 1/8
```

Ten distinct functions occupy the twelve slots, φ_LU and φ_LS sharing their first
coordinate on each objective. Every one is a quadratic of the form
g_k(x) = ½ x^T H_k x + b_k^T x + const with **constant diagonal** Hessian:

| φ | H_1 | H_2 | H_3 | H_4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LU | diag(2, 3/2) | diag(2, 5/2) | diag(3/2, 2) | diag(5/2, 2) |
| LS | diag(2, 3/2) | diag(0, 1) | diag(3/2, 2) | diag(1, 0) |
| CW | diag(2, 2) | diag(0, 1/2) | diag(2, 2) | diag(1/2, 0) |

with b_1 = (0, −2), b_3 = (−2, −2) throughout, b_2 = (0, −2) and b_4 = (−2, −2)
under φ_LU, and b_2 = b_4 = 0 under φ_LS and φ_CW.

Every Hessian is positive semidefinite, so by **Theorem 3.3** F is φ-convex under
all three φ, globally on ℝ² and a fortiori on the box, and Remark 2.2's pointwise
form holds at every point. Four of the twelve are semidefinite rather than
definite: they are the width coordinates, each affine — in fact constant — in the
variable it does not depend on, so each has a stationary *line* and not a
stationary point. That degenerate direction is exactly what produces the singular
weights of section 4.5, and it is why φ_LU has none: no image coordinate of φ_LU
is a pure width.

The convexity verdicts were confirmed twice, by the minimum eigenvalue of the
analytic Hessian and by a 20 000-chord midpoint convexity test over the box, in
which every violation is negative, i.e. convexity holding with slack.

**Regularity.** [10] (Stefanini, Arana-Jiménez and Sorini, 2025) gives a
computable criterion: F = (ĉ; r̃) is Fréchet gH-differentiable at x^{(0)} iff ĉ is
Fréchet differentiable and r̃ is abs-differentiable there. For p1 both centres are
polynomials and both half-widths are polynomials with minimum 1/8 > 0, so
|r̃| = r̃ is a polynomial and the criterion holds at every point of ℝ². **The
theorem number is cited to a literature summary and not to the paper**, which the
repository does not hold; that is row p-05 and it is the one unverified number in
this derivation. The outcome does not depend on it, and independently of [10] the
hypothesis Example 3.9 actually states — differentiability of Λ_i^T f and B_i^T f
— holds by inspection, all twelve being degree-2 polynomials.

### 4.2 What condition (15) becomes, and the map w ↦ x(w)

For quadratics with constant Hessians, condition (15) reads

> ( Σ_k w_k H_k ) x = − Σ_k w_k b_k,

a linear system in x whose coefficients are linear in w. Since every H_k is
diagonal, so is Σ_k w_k H_k, and the system splits into two scalar equations

> d_1(w) x_1 = ϱ_1(w),   d_2(w) x_2 = ϱ_2(w),

with the four linear forms read off the Hessians and the linear parts:

| φ | d_1(w) | d_2(w) | ϱ_1(w) | ϱ_2(w) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LU | 2w_1 + 2w_2 + (3/2)w_3 + (5/2)w_4 | (3/2)w_1 + (5/2)w_2 + 2w_3 + 2w_4 | 2w_3 + 2w_4 | 2w_1 + 2w_2 + 2w_3 + 2w_4 |
| LS | 2w_1 + (3/2)w_3 + w_4 | (3/2)w_1 + w_2 + 2w_3 | 2w_3 | 2w_1 + 2w_3 |
| CW | 2w_1 + 2w_3 + (1/2)w_4 | 2w_1 + (1/2)w_2 + 2w_3 | 2w_3 | 2w_1 + 2w_3 |

Wherever d_1 and d_2 are both non-zero — for w ⩾ 0 that means both positive — the
system has the unique solution

```
φ_LU   x_1(w) = (2w_3 + 2w_4) / (2w_1 + 2w_2 + (3/2)w_3 + (5/2)w_4)
       x_2(w) = (2w_1 + 2w_2 + 2w_3 + 2w_4) / ((3/2)w_1 + (5/2)w_2 + 2w_3 + 2w_4)

φ_LS   x_1(w) = 2w_3 / (2w_1 + (3/2)w_3 + w_4)
       x_2(w) = (2w_1 + 2w_3) / ((3/2)w_1 + w_2 + 2w_3)

φ_CW   x_1(w) = 2w_3 / (2w_1 + 2w_3 + (1/2)w_4)
       x_2(w) = (2w_1 + 2w_3) / (2w_1 + (1/2)w_2 + 2w_3)
```

(15) is homogeneous of degree one in w, so x(w) depends on the ray through w and
not on its length. Substituting x(w) back into the gradient of Σ_k w_k g_k and
evaluating in exact rational arithmetic on 3000 random integer weight vectors per
φ gives the zero vector exactly, not approximately.

### 4.3 The three sets, in closed form

Eliminating the weights — the parametrisations decouple, which is what makes the
region computable rather than merely samplable — gives

```
X_LU = { (x_1, x_2) : x_1 ⩾ 0,  4x_1 − x_1x_2 − 20x_2 + 16 ⩽ 0,
                                7x_1x_2 − 4x_1 + 12x_2 − 16 ⩽ 0 }
```

a region bounded below by x_1 = (20x_2 − 16)/(4 − x_2) for x_2 ∈ [4/5, 1] and
above by x_1 = (16 − 12x_2)/(7x_2 − 4) for x_2 ∈ [1, 4/3], the two conic arcs
meeting at (4/3, 1) and both reaching x_1 = 0, at x_2 = 4/5 and x_2 = 4/3;

```
X_LS = { (x_1, x_2) : 0 ⩽ x_1 ⩽ 4/3, 0 ⩽ x_2 ⩽ 4/3,
                                7x_1x_2 − 4x_1 + 12x_2 − 16 ⩽ 0 }
         minus the open segment { (x_1, 0) : 0 < x_1 ⩽ 4/3 }
```

whose upper boundary is the same conic arc that bounds X_LU above; and

> **X_CW = [0, 1] × [0, 1] minus the open segment { (x_1, 0) : 0 < x_1 ⩽ 1 }**

the closed unit square up to one edge, and the only one of the three that is a
product of intervals. Their areas, available in closed form because the
boundaries are:

> |X_LU| = 0.310533,  |X_LS| = 1.513401,  |X_CW| = 1,

against the box's area of 4 — a twelfth, three eighths and a quarter. Section 5
is where those numbers turned out to matter.

The four extreme values 4/5, 1, 4/3 and 4/3 are exactly the stationary points of
c + r, c, c − r and r in their own width variable, at 1/(1+ρ), 1, 1/(1−ρ) and 0
with ρ = 1/4. The derivation puts them where an independent grid search had
already found them.

**How much the order changes the answer, in closed form.** The areas say the
three sets are of different sizes; what the study is about is how much of one is
the other, and for **the pair that is nested in neither direction** — Example 2.2
against Example 2.4 — that is available exactly. X_LU is the set of (x_1, x_2)
with 0 ⩽ x_1 ⩽ min(U(x_2), V(x_2)), where U(x_2) = (20x_2 − 16)/(4 − x_2) and
V(x_2) = (16 − 12x_2)/(7x_2 − 4) are the two conic arcs, with U ⩽ V on [4/5, 1]
and V ⩽ U on [1, 4/3]. Intersecting with X_CW = [0, 1]² keeps x_2 ∈ [4/5, 1],
where the binding arc is U, and caps x_1 at 1, which binds for x_2 ⩾ 20/21 since
U(x_2) ⩽ 1 ⟺ x_2 ⩽ 20/21. Since 20(4 − x_2) − 64 ln(4 − x_2) is an antiderivative
of U,

```
|X_LU ∩ X_CW| = ∫_{4/5}^{20/21} U(x_2) dx_2 + (1 − 20/21)
              = 64 ln(21/20) − 3
              = 0.1225705068…
```

Checked against adaptive quadrature, which agrees to 3.0e−15, and against a count
of an 8000 × 8000 grid of the decision box, which gives 0.122571.

| pair | share of the first lying in the second | share of the second lying in the first | shared fraction of the union |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ex. 2.2 against Ex. 2.4** | **0.394710** | **0.122571** | **0.103177** |
| Ex. 2.2 against Ex. 2.3 | 1 *(by the containment)* | 0.205189 | 0.205189 |
| Ex. 2.4 against Ex. 2.3 | 1 *(by the containment)* | 0.660763 | 0.660763 |

Read on the top row, which is the only one free in both directions: **changing
the order from Example 2.2 to Example 2.4 on the same problem, 60.5% of what was
optimal stops being optimal and 87.7% of the new optimal set was not optimal
before. The two sets share 10.3% of their union.** That is the sentence this
study exists to make, and for p1 it is exact rather than measured.

Four things bound it, and none of them is a caveat about the arithmetic.

*It is a statement about area*, Lebesgue measure in the decision plane, and not a
count of points. The sets are two-dimensional, §3.3, so measure is the natural
size; a count would be a property of whatever sampled them.

*It is about the derived sets and not about anything a solver returned.* It is
what an exact solver under each order would deliver. What the experiments add is
the same quantity computed on **recovered** sets, with variance across seeds,
which is the number the memoria reports and which no derivation supplies.

*It measures change and does not rank.* Neither set is better; they are different
answers to the same problem under two different orders, and which order a decision
maker wants is settled by the coefficients they choose, §2.1, not by this number.

*The two lower rows are a different kind of statement.* One direction there is 1
before any arithmetic, by the containment of §6.1, so those rows are a check on
that containment and the top row is the finding. This is the same instruction the
experimental tables carry.

**Interiority.** X_LU ⊆ [0, 4/3] × [4/5, 4/3] and X_LS, X_CW ⊆ [0, 4/3]². The
box is [−1/2, 3/2]² and 4/3 < 3/2, so every point of all three derived sets is
strictly interior with a margin of at least 1/6 on every side, and Example 3.9
applies to all of them. This is the property ρ and the box were chosen to obtain,
and it is confirmed rather than assumed.

**Verification, both directions, in exact rational arithmetic.** Forward, on the
weight simplex at denominator 40, 12 341 vectors per φ: 0 landed outside the
stated region, with 2 singular vectors for φ_LS and φ_CW and none for φ_LU.
Backward, inverting the parametrisation at 301 random rational points of each
region: 0 without a witness weight. Against an independent 61 × 61 grid of the
box filtered by the ordinary Pareto relation: 0 of 20 000 sampled derived points
are dominated by any grid point, under any φ. The residue in the other direction
— grid points outside the region — is the finite grid and was diagnosed as such
on four independent pieces of evidence, the decisive one being that a 24-fold
local refinement drops 153 of 171 and 76 of 87 of them while dropping 0 of the
289, 1418 and 961 in-region points.

### 4.4 Optimal against weakly optimal, by the shortest published route

**Every x(w) at a non-singular w is an *optimal* solution**, and the route is
**Example 3.8 statement 3**, not Example 3.9. The scalarised objective
Σ_k w_k g_k is a quadratic whose Hessian Σ_k w_k H_k is positive definite exactly
when the system is non-singular, so it is strictly convex; x(w) is its stationary
point, hence its unique global minimiser over ℝ², and since x(w) is interior to
the box it is the unique minimiser over S. Statement 3's hypothesis is exactly
uniqueness, with no positivity of the weights and no convexity of F required, so
it carries the conclusion all the way to optimality including at weight vectors
with zero components. That is strictly stronger than Example 3.9 statement 3,
which would deliver optimality only where every weight is strictly positive.
Example 3.9 statement 3 was used as a cross-check and agrees where it applies.

**Completeness, i.e. the reverse containment.** Sufficiency alone would only say
the derived set is contained in the efficient set. **Example 3.9 statement 1** is
a necessary condition whose only hypothesis is differentiability of the image
coordinates, which holds everywhere for p1, so for interior x̄:

```
x̄ weakly optimal ⇒ (15) holds at x̄ for some w ⩾ 0, w ≠ 0
                  ⇒ x̄ ∈ X_φ, or x̄ lies on one of the two singular lines.
```

Combining with Definition 3.1's implication chain:

> **X_φ ⊆ optimal set ⊆ weak optimal set ⊆ X_φ ∪ (the singular lines)**

and for φ_LU there are no singular lines, so the sandwich collapses: **under φ_LU
the optimal set and the weak optimal set both equal X_LU exactly, among interior
points.**

### 4.5 What did not close, and why it was left open

Under φ_LS and φ_CW the matrix Σ_k w_k H_k is singular on exactly two weight rays,
w = (0, w_2, 0, 0) and w = (0, 0, 0, w_4) with the free weight positive: the two
vectors putting all their mass on a single width coordinate. In both cases the
vanishing row comes with a vanishing right-hand side, so the system is
**consistent** and has a line of solutions rather than none — x_2 = 0 in the first
case, x_1 = 0 in the second. That is not an accident of the numbers: each width
coordinate has b_k = 0, so a support that kills a row of the matrix kills the
matching entry of the right-hand side with it. There is no weight, under any of
the three φ, for which the system is inconsistent.

On those rays **the published conditions give weak optimality and no optimality
verdict either way**. Weak optimality comes from Example 3.8 statement 1, which
needs no differentiability and no unambiguous weight reading: every point of the
line minimises the corresponding width coordinate globally. But Example 3.8
statement 3 does not apply, the scalar problem there having a line of minimisers
rather than one; Example 3.8 statement 2 and Example 3.9 statement 3 both require
every weight strictly positive, which the singular rays do not have; and no
regular weight reaches the interior of the segments, since x_2(w) = 0 forces
w_1 = w_3 = 0. The undecided sets are

> { (x_1, 0) : 0 < x_1 ⩽ 4/3 } under φ_LS,   { (x_1, 0) : 0 < x_1 ⩽ 1 } under φ_CW,

together with their reflections on the line x_1 = 0, and they are exactly the
closure of X_LS and X_CW minus the sets themselves.

**The derivation stops there and is not patched.** Every measurement the project
has made says those points are non-dominated — a 61 × 61 grid, and dense random
samples of 50 000 box points at three seeds, in which 0 of 2000 front points are
dominated. So the measurement and the theory do not disagree; the theory is
silent, and closing the gap would mean inventing a condition. What was done
instead is that the reference front takes a flag naming which of the two sets
is meant, **with no default**, so that whoever computes a metric has to say so,
and the value is recorded in every table beside the seed and the point count. The
cost of not knowing was priced rather than argued: with the region sample held
fixed and the segment added on top, the IGD of a fixed test front moves by
−0.63% to +5.36% at 1000 reference points and by −0.11% to +1.35% at 20 000. So
the choice cannot flip a comparison that is not already within a few per cent,
and it can flip one that is. This is question Q3 of section 11, row s-12.

### 4.6 A witness bearing on the weight-phrase ambiguity

Take φ_LU and x̄ = (4/3, 1), which is interior to the box. Condition (15) at x̄ is
the homogeneous system A w = 0 with

> A = [ [ 8/3, 8/3, 0, 4/3 ], [ −1/2, 1/2, 0, 0 ] ],

and on the simplex grid at denominator 100 exactly one weight vector satisfies
it, w = (0, 0, 1, 0), which has three zero components; none with every component
strictly positive does. Meanwhile x̄ is the unique global minimiser of
g_3 = c_2 − r_2, whose Hessian diag(3/2, 2) is positive definite, so Example 3.8
statement 3 makes x̄ an optimal solution and hence, by the implication chain, a
weak optimal solution. A search over 2.2 million points of the box, 200 000 of
them within 10^{−4} of x̄, found nothing dominating it.

**So under the strict reading of Example 3.9's weight phrase, statement 1 would
be false at this point for this F.** This is evidence and not a resolution: it is
one instance and it rests on the project's reading of Example 3.8 statement 3.
Row s-02, question Q4. Nothing in section 4 depends on the answer, because every
optimality conclusion is drawn from Example 3.8, whose weight condition is
unambiguous. The one place the strict reading would bite is the necessity
direction of 4.4, which would then be unavailable and would leave the derived
sets proved to be *contained in* the efficient set but not proved to exhaust it.

### 4.7 p0, per φ

| hypothesis | φ_LU | φ_LS | φ_CW |

| Example 3.8 statements 1, 2, 3 | available | available | available |

The single failing convexity coordinate is Λ_1^T f = −|x|, which is concave; under

**The published anchor is recovered.** Under each φ the scalarised problem (14)
has x = 0 as its unique minimiser for suitable weights — for instance

## 5. The solvers, and the validation gate

### 5.1 What was built

Three solvers on one contract, each returning per seed the front as a (k, 2m)
array and the matching (k, n) array of decision vectors. The decision vectors are
not optional: the only metrics comparable across φ are computed on them, because
the decision space is the one space every φ shares, whereas hypervolume under two
φ is a volume measured in two different spaces.

*Random search*, the *referencia base* of slide 17. Sampling takes no φ, so a
sample is a function of the box, the budget and the seed alone; one sample is
evaluated once and filtered under each φ in turn. This is the φ-neutral
instrument.

*NSGA-II* and *MOPSO_CD*, both from `pymoo` 0.6.2 and used as published. Two
details of the harness are worth naming because both were defects that were
measured and fixed. Budget parity: under `pymoo`'s generation-count termination
MOPSO evaluates one population more than NSGA-II — 80, 120 and 220 against 60,
100 and 200 at three settings — because its initial swarm is evaluated outside
the generation count, so the budget is stated in evaluations instead and both
then report exactly pop_size × n_gen. And reproducibility: `pymoo` 0.6.2
truncates an overflowing archive with a generator that neither seeding call
reaches, so five runs of MOPSO at one seed gave five different fronts; section
7.7.

One dominance relation is used everywhere — the ordinary Pareto relation on
doubles, with no rounding, no epsilon and no tolerance anywhere in the project.
`pymoo`'s own non-domination and the project's were checked to agree exactly on
solver output, on all four problems under all three φ, twenty-four cases with no
row differing.

### 5.2 What the gate asserts, and what it only reports

The gate runs all three solvers on p1 under all three φ, at five seeds and both
settings of the singular-segment flag: ninety measurements. It **asserts** two
directions.

*Reference to solver*, the convergence question: the largest distance from a
point of the derived set to the recovered set, in the decision space, within a
stated tolerance, per solver, φ, seed and flag setting.

*No solver point dominates the derived set*: the count of reference front points
that some solver front point dominates is zero. This is the direction that would
indict the derivation of section 4 or its encoding.

It **reports and never asserts** the forward direction, solver to reference, and
the reason is a proposition rather than an observation; section 5.5. It also
reports the exact quality measure — the fraction of solver front points outside
the closed-form region of section 4.3, which needs no sample and which no
sampling density can move — the budget trend, and the front cardinality beside
every number.

**The tolerance, derived before the runs and never adjusted after them.** It is
the 0.95 quantile of the fill distance, with respect to the derived region R, of
a uniform k-point draw of R, at k = 100, over 1000 independent draws. k = 100 is
the smallest front size the design fixes in advance. Both the quantile and the
cardinality were written down before the study executed, with the reading fixed
in advance too: at the 0.95 quantile a solver whose front were a uniform draw
would exceed the tolerance in about one measurement in twenty, so **one isolated
failure at a margin near zero is not evidence of a defect, while a failure
concentrated in one solver across seeds and φ is.**

| φ | tolerance | as a fraction of the box side (2) | uniform mean at k = 100 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LU | 0.2405 | 12.0% | 0.1420 |
| LS | 0.2919 | 14.6% | 0.2232 |
| CW | 0.2318 | 11.6% | 0.1783 |

The tolerance is coarse and the reason is structural rather than a concession:
the derived sets are two-dimensional regions of substantial area, and a
hundred-point front cannot cover a region of a quarter or three eighths of the
box to better than 0.13 to 0.22 at the median. A sharper gate on p1 is not
available at this budget from any solver, and every margin is printed so that a
pass with no margin is visible as one.

### 5.4 The finding those twelve carry, with its magnitude

**For a full-dimensional efficient set, NSGA-II's decision-space coverage is
worse than uniform random sampling of that set at equal cardinality, under φ_LS
and φ_CW and not under φ_LU.** NSGA-II returns exactly 100 rows, which is exactly
the k the tolerance's distribution is measured at, so the comparison needs no
correction. Where each run's fill distance sits in the distribution of 1000
uniform 100-point draws of the same region:

| solver | φ | k | percentile, five seeds | worse than the uniform mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NSGA-II | LU | 100 | 33, 49, 47, 60, 45 | 0 of 5 |
| NSGA-II | LS | 100 | 98, 89, 98, 97, 85 | 5 of 5 |
| NSGA-II | CW | 100 | 95, 98, 99, 98, 90 | 5 of 5 |
| MOPSO | LS | 200 | 89, 30, 97, 68, 77 | 4 of 5 |
| MOPSO | CW | 200 | 43, 41, 85, 54, 86 | 2 of 5 |
| MOPSO | LU | 200 | 55, 43, 18, 61, 36 | 0 of 5 |

What it does **not** say: it is not that NSGA-II fails to converge, since no
solver returns a point beating the derivation anywhere; and it is not a result
about φ.

**About half of it is not about NSGA-II at all.** Random search is φ-neutral by
construction, and at matched cardinality — twenty uniform 100-row subsamples of
each random-search front, read against the same distribution — it shows no
deficit under any φ: 12.7…29.4 under φ_LU, 21.4…63.2 under φ_LS, 31.1…48.7 under

at the same k it carries **half** the swing from φ_LU to each of the other two:

| swing | NSGA-II | random search | share carried by the φ-neutral control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LU → LS | +0.483 | +0.243 | 50% |
| LU → CW | +0.485 | +0.244 | 50% |

That half is **region shape acting on the comparator**. In units of √(|R|/k),
which divides region size out, a uniform draw covers X_LU 40% worse for its area
than it covers X_LS or X_CW, because X_LU is a thin curved sliver between two
conic arcs and the other two are fat. The yardstick is weakest exactly where
NSGA-II looks best. The two halves agreeing to three decimal places is a
coincidence of two numbers and is read as nothing.

**Five candidate mechanisms for the residual half were put to direct measurement
and all five are excluded.**

1. *Rank-1 saturation.* Refuted because it fires under **every** φ, section 6.3, and a mechanism present under all three cannot produce a result that appears under two.
2. *Wasted front slots.* Re-reading each percentile at the number of front rows within 0.10 of R buys 2 to 11 percentile points of a gap of 50 and leaves all ten failing measurements at or above the 74th percentile. Only a cut discarding a third of the front moves the number, and that describes the front rather than correcting the reading of it.
3. *That NSGA-II is bad at what it optimises.* It is not. Measured in the 2m image space it actually sorts and spreads in, each column normalised by its range as the crowding distance normalises it, NSGA-II sits at the 17th–86th percentile under φ_LS and the 31st–71st under φ_CW — where a uniform draw itself sits — while under φ_LU it is at the 0.1st–4.9th, better than 95% of them. The deficit is in the pullback.
4. *Anisotropy of the map.* Under the column-normalised Jacobian the ordering is the reverse of what a distortion story needs: φ_LU is the anisotropic one, median singular-value ratio 3.97 against 1.42 and 1.45, and no ratio anywhere exceeds 10. Section 6.2 then makes the φ_LU-versus-φ_CW part of that table an artefact of averaging over two different regions.
5. *Alignment of the crowding distance's axes.* A front produced under one φ can be read in another's frame, the three images being constant linear maps of one another. The advantage travels with the run and not with the frame: a φ_CW front read in φ_LU's frame sits at the 44th–81st percentile, nowhere near the φ_LU runs' 0.1–4.9. And the operator behaves identically under all three φ — the coefficient of variation of the crowding distance is lowest in the run's own frame in all fifteen runs, at 0.25–0.32, 0.24–0.43 and 0.25–0.30.

**The residual half is recorded as unexplained and no mechanism is offered for
it.** It is a real, reproducible, correctly measured property of one solver on one
problem at one cardinality over five seeds, and it is what the twelve gate
failures are.

### 5.5 Why the other direction is reported and never asserted

There is a proposition here, and it is the reason a first version of the gate
asserted something no solver can achieve.

**Proposition.** Let S be any finite set of decision vectors in p1's box, with at
least two elements, and let x* ∈ S satisfy |x*_1| < |y_1| for every other y ∈ S.
Then under φ_LS and under φ_CW no element of S dominates x*, whatever x*_2 is.
The same holds for the element of strictly smallest |x_2|.

**Proof.** Under φ_CW the fourth image column is g_4 = r_2 and under φ_LS it is
g_4 = 2r_2, so in both cases g_4(x) = a(ρx_1² + δ) with a > 0, a strictly
increasing function of |x_1| and of |x_1| alone: it does not contain x_2. By
hypothesis g_4(x*) < g_4(y) for every other y ∈ S. Domination of x* by y requires
g_j(y) ⩽ g_j(x*) in every column, and at j = 4 that contradicts the strict
inequality. Nothing in the argument mentions x*_2, so x*_2 is unconstrained. The
second column is a(ρx_2² + δ) and gives the |x_2| statement. Under φ_LU every one
of the four columns moves with both variables and the hypothesis has nothing to
stand on. ∎

**Corollary.** Ordering S by |x_1| ascending, the j-th element can be dominated
only by one of the j−1 below it. So the whole low-|x_1| tail is over-represented
in the front and its other coordinate is unconstrained throughout.

The consequence is that the forward distance, solver to reference, has a **floor
no budget crosses**: the protected point's x_2 ranges over [−1/2, 3/2] while
X_CW's ranges over [0, 1], so for a uniform draw it lies outside with probability
exactly 1/2 and its expected overhang is exactly 1/8, at every budget. Measured
over 200 uniform draws at four sizes, the median smallest |x_1| falls by a factor
of four for every factor of four in n — 7.45e−04, 1.41e−04, 4.21e−05, 1.00e−05 —
while the mean overhang does not move at all: 0.1226, 0.1404, 0.1334, 0.1261.

This is a property of the design and not a defect of any solver: the proposition
is about an arbitrary finite set, and holds for a uniform draw, for an NSGA-II
population, and for the output of a solver that had converged perfectly and then
filtered its own evaluations. It is also why **no monotone trend in budget is
asserted of any solver on this problem**: a larger budget elects a new protected
member of smaller |x_1| at an x_2 that is no better placed. Measured: four times
the budget lowers the outside fraction for NSGA-II and MOPSO under φ_LU by about
two points and does not lower it under φ_LS or φ_CW, and NSGA-II's twelve gate
failures are unchanged in magnitude at four times the budget.

## 6. Three findings about the framework rather than about this project

These are the results of phase c that concern [1] itself. They are offered for
comment; two of them the project does not build on.

### 6.1 A containment between the solution sets of two of your automorphisms

**The criterion.** Let φ_A and φ_B be two admissible automorphisms of the class,
and let M be the 2 × 2 real matrix with φ_B = M φ_A on each interval objective. M
is not a choice: each φ is invertible, admissibility being exactly a non-zero
determinant, so M = φ_B φ_A^{−1} is determined by the ordered pair. **If every
entry of M is non-negative and M is invertible, then φ_A-dominance implies
φ_B-dominance, and therefore ND_B(S) ⊆ ND_A(S), for every S and every F.**

The proof is two lines. If x dominates y under φ_A then d = φ_A(F(y)) −
φ_A(F(x)) satisfies d ⩾ 0 componentwise and d ≠ 0. Non-negativity of M gives
Md ⩾ 0, since every entry of Md is a sum of products of non-negative numbers;
invertibility gives Md ≠ 0, so the strictness cannot be cancelled. Together those
are φ_B-dominance. Taking the contrapositive gives the containment. No convexity,
no differentiability and no structure on S is used, and the sign of a half-width
is never used either: the argument is about the two image spaces and the map
between them, not about F.

**The criterion itself is standard** — it is the elementary observation that a
non-negative injective linear map preserves the Pareto order — and it is not what
is being reported. **What is being reported is its application to your family.**
The six maps between the three implemented φ, each computed as φ_B φ_A^{−1}:

| from | to | M | det | entrywise non-negative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| φ_LS | φ_LU | [[1, 0], [1, 1]] | 1 | **yes** |
| φ_LS | φ_CW | [[1, 1/2], [0, 1/2]] | 1/2 | **yes** |
| φ_LU | φ_LS | [[1, 0], [−1, 1]] | 1 | no |
| φ_LU | φ_CW | [[1/2, 1/2], [−1/2, 1/2]] | 1/2 | no |
| φ_CW | φ_LS | [[1, −1], [0, 2]] | 2 | no |
| φ_CW | φ_LU | [[1, −1], [1, 1]] | 2 | no |

Exactly two of the six qualify, both out of φ_LS, so

> **ND_LU(S) ⊆ ND_LS(S)  and  ND_CW(S) ⊆ ND_LS(S), exactly and for every problem,**

and φ_LU against φ_CW is nested in neither direction. Note the direction:
φ_LS-dominance implies the other two, so ≦_{φ_LS} is the **weakest** of the three
relations and its non-dominated set is correspondingly the **largest**.

**The consequence for what this study may claim**, which is why it matters here
and not only as a curiosity. On the φ_LS–φ_LU pair and on the φ_LS–φ_CW pair, one
direction of every coverage and overlap statistic is fixed before a solver is
run, so a difference measured there is in part a theorem and must not be reported
as evidence that the efficient set is sensitive to the order. **The comparison
that carries the sensitivity signal is Example 2.2 against Example 2.4**, which
are nested in neither direction, and the study's headline number will come from
that pair, measured on random search output so that the search is identical by
construction. On p1 that pair already has an exact answer, §4.3: the two derived
sets share 10.3% of their union, and moving between the two orders costs 60.5% of
the optimal set and gains 87.7% of a new one. The other two pairs are reported as checks with the containment
named.

**Relation to your Proposition 5.1.** The φ_LU case is the interval-space
analogue of Proposition 5.1, printed page 19, lines 1481–1487, which states for
the **fuzzy** problems that x̄ optimal for (1MFIOP_φ) with φ of Example 2.2
implies x̄ optimal for (1MFIOP_ψ) with ψ of Example 2.3 — so Opt(2.2) ⊆ Opt(2.3),
the Example 2.3 set the larger one, with no convexity, no differentiability and
no converse. The correspondence is exact in three respects: the same two
automorphisms in the same direction giving the same containment, the same absence
of hypotheses, and the same one-directionality. It is **not** derived from
Proposition 5.1 and is not a corollary of it: the fuzzy result is about fuzzy
problems, and Theorem 5.1 is what relates the two formulations; nothing here goes
through either. Sections 2 and 3 of [1] were searched exhaustively and contain no
stated relation between the solution sets of two automorphisms, and Example 2.4
appears in no such statement anywhere in the paper.

**Three things are asked about this, and they are question Q6.** Is the criterion
correct as stated? Are both containments correct? And is any of it already
published — [1]'s own [26], cited for LS-convexity and LS-Pareto, is where the
φ_LU statement would sit, with [9] and [31] the other candidates, and none of
the three can be resolved because the reference list is missing from the text the
project holds. The third question matters most: if it is published, the memoria
cites it; if it is not, the memoria carries a small original observation, and a
project whose own rules forbid it from proving theorems should hand that to its
supervisors rather than present it on its own authority. **Until then the project
builds nothing on it**: the derivation of section 4 reproduces both containments
from the closed forms by an entirely independent route, which is a check on the
derivation and not evidence for the criterion.

### 6.2 The three images are constant linear images of one another

Each φ is a constant linear map of the endpoint pair, so for any decision set the
three transformed images are constant linear images of one another. Writing g_LU,
g_LS and g_CW for the 2m image columns of one problem,

```
g_LU = A g_CW,  A = [[1, −1, 0, 0], [1, 1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1, −1], [0, 0, 1, 1]]
g_LS = B g_CW,  B = [[1, −1, 0, 0], [0, 2, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1, −1], [0, 0, 0, 2]]
```

**A^T A = 2I**, so A is √2 times an orthogonal matrix: all four singular values
are √2 and the condition number is exactly 1. **Examples 2.2 and 2.4 present the
same geometric object in two alignments**, differing by a rotation and a uniform
scaling and nothing else.

**B is not a similarity.** B^T B is block diagonal with two copies of
[[1, −1], [−1, 5]], whose eigenvalues are 3 ± √5, so B's singular values are
√(3+√5) = 2.288246 and √(3−√5) = 0.874032, each twice, and its condition number
is exactly

> (1 + √5)² / 4 = 2.618034,

the golden ratio squared. The map from the φ_LS image to the φ_LU image has
singular values exactly the golden ratio and its reciprocal, and the same
condition number.

**A and B carry no parameter of any problem.** They contain no ρ, no δ, no box
and no objective; they are built from the coefficient pairs of Examples 2.2, 2.3
and 2.4 alone. So the relations hold for every interval problem in the framework
under these three φ. Verified to 0.000e+00 on 500 random points and on all three
region samples.

Two remarks, both consistency checks rather than new claims. This does not
contradict 6.1: dominance is preserved by an entrywise non-negative map, not by
an arbitrary invertible one, and the per-objective blocks of A and B both carry a
negative entry — which is exactly why φ_LU and φ_CW are nested in neither
direction while both sit inside φ_LS. The two facts describe different things,
one the geometry a solver's operators see and the other which points are
efficient. And it forces a negative statement the project needed: **no claim of
the form "the φ_LU map distorts more than the φ_CW map" can be true**, since
pointwise they are the same map up to a similarity, so any measurement appearing
to show one is measuring the region it was averaged over.

### 6.3 The transformation costs dominance-based selection its pressure

Doubling m to 2m puts even a two-objective interval problem into the
many-objective regime, and this was measured rather than argued.

NSGA-II sorts pop_size + offspring = 200 candidates a generation and keeps 100.
If rank 1 alone holds at least 100 of them, dominance never enters the selection:
every survivor is chosen on crowding distance, and the algorithm is a spread
maximiser with no convergence pressure. The rank-1 size was read out of the
algorithm's state after every generation through a callback, with `pymoo`
unmodified.

| problem | real columns | φ | rank 1 at gen 1 | first gen with rank 1 ⩾ 100 | rank 1 over gens 21–50, median |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| p1 | 4 | LU | 19–26 | 4 at every seed | 168–172 of 200 |
| p1 | 4 | LS | 57–65 | 3, 3, 3, 3, 2 | 170–173 |
| p1 | 4 | CW | 46–54 | 3, 4, 3, 3, 3 | 167–170 |
| ZDT1 | 4 | LU / LS / CW | 16 / 29 / 26 | 14 / 13 / 21 | 124 / 131 / 118 |
| DTLZ2 | 6 | LU / LS / CW | 50 / 64 / 56 | 4 / 3 / 3 | 146 / 155 / 153 |

**It saturates in all nine problem–φ configurations, and never falls back** — on
p1 at generation 3 or 4 at every one of the five seeds, so for 46 or 47 of the 50
generations dominance decides nothing. `pymoo` stops its survival sort as soon as
the ranked count reaches the number of survivors, so from that point it
enumerates a *single* front where four or five exist.

Two things this does and does not say. It is **a cost of the transformation**,
not a misconfiguration of any solver: at 2m objectives the non-dominated fraction
of a sample rises far enough that a dominance-based survival operator has nothing
left to discriminate with. It is the empirical form of the objection that
direct-interval methods raise against transformation-based ones, stated as a
measurement on this project's own problems rather than as a citation. And it does
**not** explain the coverage finding of 5.4, because a mechanism that fires
equally under all three φ cannot produce a result appearing under two of them and
not the third — which is exactly how the hypothesis was refuted.

What orders the three problems is **not** the column count: p1 transforms to four
columns and saturates as fast as DTLZ2's six, while ZDT1 also transforms to four
and takes 13 to 21 generations. What tracks the ordering is the non-dominated
fraction of the transformed image — 0.58, 0.79 and 0.55 on DTLZ2 against 0.24,
0.52 and 0.47 on ZDT1. Three problems is not enough to assert that as a law and
it is not asserted; every experimental configuration will therefore report the
number rather than predict it.

## 7. Errors found and corrected

Eight, in the shape: what was wrong, how it was caught, what it would have cost,
what was done. They are here because the project's confidence in its remaining
numbers rests on the fact that these were found, and most of them were found by
one discipline — that a coefficient, a number or a theorem statement enters the
project only from a paper read in this project and recorded with its location,
and that a suspicion is measured before it is believed.

### 7.1 A third φ that is not in the paper

*What was wrong.* The project carried a third automorphism, defined as
(f̄, half-width) with λ = (0, 1) and β = (−1/2, 1/2), which appears nowhere in [1].
It had been carried with an "unverified" flag rather than left out.

*How it was caught.* By reading [1] and enumerating the named examples. The
paper's third named example carrying a convexity notion is Example 2.3, λ = (1, 0),
β = (−1, 1), giving the lower endpoint and the **full** width — a different order.

*What it would have cost.* An experiment variable with one of its three values
not in the framework it claims to be exploring, and every downstream number about
it meaningless.

*What was done.* The φ was removed, before the repository existed, which is why
the repository holds no trace of it. What the project added afterwards is the
verification pass of section 2 and the rule the episode produced: **an unverified
item is not flagged and kept, it is left out until it is verified.** A flag is a
note about something already established; it is not a substitute for checking.
Every other item in this section was found because that rule was being followed.

### 7.2 The constant-width degeneracy

*What was wrong.* The literal reading of slide 19, F_i = [f_i − ε, f_i + ε], makes
all three φ coincide with each other and with the crisp order, so the study would
have measured nothing.

*How it was caught.* By running the check before building anything on the model:
sample the box, compute centre and width per objective, and filter the same
sample under each φ. In exact arithmetic the four non-dominated sets came back
**identical as index sets**, on both benchmarks at both levels tested.

*What it would have cost.* A complete study, correctly executed, of a construction
that cannot show a difference. And it would have been reported as a null result
about φ rather than as a defect of the model.

*What was done.* The half-width became a function of the decision vector, on the
rule of section 3.2, and the check became a standing requirement run on every new
problem before use — including, when it arrives, the portfolio problem.

### 7.3 A ZDT1 width whose optimum coincides with g's

*What was wrong.* The natural half-width for ZDT1, r = ε x_n, passes every
width-versus-centre statistic on a uniform sample of the 30-dimensional box and
is degenerate: on the slice where ZDT1's efficient set actually lives, φ_CW
reproduces the crisp efficient set **exactly**, 81 of 6561, while φ_LU and φ_LS
return the whole slice. The cause is alignment: g is minimised at x_n = 0 and so
is the width, so nothing trades.

*How it was caught.* By re-running the diagnostic on the slice rather than on the
box. A uniform sample of a 30-dimensional box contains essentially nothing near
the efficient set, so the box-level diagnostic reported separation where the
efficient sets coincide.

*What it would have cost.* Tier 1 would have measured nothing while appearing to
measure something, and the failure would have surfaced only at the synthesis,
after both benchmark sweeps had been run.

*What was done.* ZDT1's half-width became quadratic in x_n, with an interior
optimum different from g's, while DTLZ2's stayed linear because its g is already
quadratic — section 3.4. The stronger check was built as an assertion in both
problem modules. And the general form of the lesson is recorded: **the
independence condition is necessary and not sufficient, and it must be checked on
the slice where the efficient set lives.**

### 7.4 Endpoint cancellation destroying the width column

*What was wrong.* The second image coordinate of φ_LS and φ_CW is a width, and
the natural way to reach it from the endpoint pair is to subtract: (c + r) −
(c − r), which is not exactly 2r in double precision.

*How it was caught.* By measuring the error against a reference computed in exact
integer arithmetic. The error is ε_mach·|c| exactly, and nothing else — on p1's
grid the maximum absolute error of the width column runs 7.2e−16, 1.1e−13,
1.1e−10 and 1.1e−07 as a constant added to both centres runs 0, 10³, 10⁶ and 10⁹,
while the same column computed from (c, r) holds 2.2e−16 at all four.

**The size of the error is not the finding.** The finding is what it does to the
*structure* of the column. p1's r_1 depends on x_2 alone, so on a 61-point axis it
takes exactly 46 distinct values, and every pair of points sharing x_2 shares r_1
**exactly**. An order built on a column is built on that column's ties. The
endpoint route shatters those 46 values into **210**; the centre–radius route
returns 46.

*What it would have cost.* Spurious non-dominated points, in a pattern that
depends on the sample and is invisible in the outputs — and, in the degenerate
case of 7.2, a reported difference between φ that is entirely rounding.

*What was done.* A tolerance was measured and then rejected. It works at p1's
magnitudes — every value from 10^{−15} to 10^{−4} returns the exact set for all
three φ, an eleven-decade plateau — but the band narrows by one decade per decade
of centre magnitude and is gone by |c| = 10¹², which is a magnitude the portfolio
application may well reach; and scaling per column by that column's own size is
**worse** than not scaling, because the noise in a width column is proportional to
the centre it was subtracted out of and not to the width. Also the tolerance
argument on `pymoo` 0.6.2's non-dominated sorting is a no-op — it subtracts the same
number from every entry, which is a translation, to which dominance is invariant
— so no tolerance could reach inside the solver anyway.

So the subtraction was removed instead. An interval's endpoint pair and its
centre–half-width pair are related by (f, f̄) = M(c, r) with
M = [[1, −1], [1, 1]], det M = 2, and composing your φ with M gives

```
first  = (λ_1 + λ_2) c + (λ_2 − λ_1) r,
second = (β_1 + β_2) c + (β_2 − β_1) r,
```

whose determinant is 2(λ_1β_2 − λ_2β_1) = 2 det φ. **It is the same automorphism
read in other coordinates, not a second family**, and it is singular exactly when
your admissibility condition fails and never otherwise; this was checked entry by
entry in exact rational arithmetic on 2000 random coefficient pairs before it was
implemented. The rule that follows is: a problem declares the representation its
intervals are actually computed in, and φ is applied once, as one linear map, to
that representation. No endpoint is ever built from a centre and a radius and
then differenced back. **The project therefore uses one dominance relation
everywhere with no tolerance, no rounding and no epsilon** — the arithmetic error
a tolerance would have had to cover is not committed in the first place. This is
row s-06: whether the centre–radius form should be presented in the memoria as
the working form of φ or as an implementation note is a presentation question and
is question Q7.

### 7.5 A tolerance that summed two terms where one belongs

*What was wrong.* The gate's tolerance was set to h_reference + h_design, the
covering radius of the derived region by the reference sample plus the covering
radius by a design-sized front, on the ground that "both sets are finite samples
of the same region and each carries its own resolution floor".

*How it was caught.* By writing the direction the gate actually asserts and
bounding it. The reverse distance is sup_{p ∈ reference} dist(p, S), and **every
reference point lies in R**, so that supremum is at most sup_{y ∈ R} dist(y, S),
the fill distance of S with respect to R. **The reference sample enters only
through being a subset of R, and its own resolution plays no part in this
direction at all.** More generally, for any two subsets A, B ⊆ R,
d_H(A, B) ⩽ max(fill(A), fill(B)) — a maximum, never a sum. Checked on 300 random
pairs of subsets of each region: the bound held 300 of 300 in both directions
under all three φ, with the slack reaching 0.0000, so it is attained and not
merely valid, while the sum stood at 0.189, 0.293 and 0.238 against maxima of
0.143, 0.219 and 0.177. About a third of the tolerance was slack that no argument
put there.

*What it would have cost.* A gate too loose to catch what it exists to catch, and
a tolerance whose verdict moved by a factor of about 1.5 with a choice nobody had
justified.

*What was done.* The tolerance became h_design alone, and h_design stopped being
a mean of twenty draws and became a measured distribution over 1000 draws read at
a quantile fixed in writing before the study ran. **The correction did not move
in one direction**: removing the reference term tightens it and reading a quantile
rather than a mean loosens it, and the net effect was looser by 0.0776 under φ_LU
and tighter by 0.0496 and 0.0895 under φ_LS and φ_CW. A tolerance adjusted to a
purpose would not have gone up for one φ and down for two.

### 7.6 A gate assertion made unachievable by a provable sampling artefact

*What was wrong.* The first version of the gate asserted the **forward**
direction, from the solver's front to the derived set, and it also asserted that
the dominated fraction does not rise when the budget is quadrupled.

*How it was caught.* By turning the mechanism into a proposition. Section 5.5 is
that proposition: under φ_LS and φ_CW one image column depends on a single
decision variable, so the sample point of strictly smallest |x_1| is the strict
minimiser of that column and **nothing can dominate it, whatever its other
coordinate is**. It enters every front the Pareto filter returns, its x_2 is
unconstrained, and a larger budget elects a new such point rather than removing
the old one. The forward distance therefore has a floor that no solver can clear
and no budget removes, and it is exactly 1/8 in expectation for a uniform draw.

*What it would have cost.* A gate that fails permanently for a reason that has
nothing to do with any solver's quality, and — worse — a reader who takes a
forward distance for a quality measure and ranks the three solvers by where their
fronts' protected extremes happened to fall.

*What was done.* The forward assertion was removed and the forward distance is
reported with the floor stated beside it. The budget assertion was removed **for
all three solvers**, not restricted to φ_LU: φ_LU is precisely the φ where the
mechanism is absent, so a trend asserted there and nowhere else would be an
assertion selected by the measurement that is supposed to judge it. The trend is
reported as a number instead. A second correction rode along with this one: the
quality measure had been "the count of front points dominated by the reference
front", which depends on the reference's density — on one run it goes from 9% at
250 reference rows to 31% at 16 000, where the exact answer is 46% — and it was
replaced by exact membership in the closed-form region of section 4.3, which
needs no sample at all.

### 7.7 A MOPSO archive resize that changed the algorithm

*What was wrong.* `pymoo` 0.6.2 truncates an overflowing archive with a generator
that neither of the two seeding calls the harness makes reaches, so MOPSO runs are
not reproducible once the archive overflows: five runs at one seed gave five
different fronts of 179, 178, 184, 194 and 189 rows, diverging at generation 17
with the algorithm's own seeded generator in an identical state. The **first fix**
was to enlarge the archive to the whole evaluation budget so that the truncation
is never reached. That works and is reproducible, and it was wrong.

*How it was caught.* By asking what the archive is. It is MOPSO's leader pool, so
resizing it from 200 to the 6295 rows a run returns at budget 20 000 **changes
what the search does**, which is a modification of a published algorithm and not a
harness fix. It also cost a factor of 16 in runtime at that budget — 187.78 s
against 11.71 s.

*What it would have cost.* A comparison in which one of the three solvers is not
the published algorithm, with the difference invisible in every table.

*What was done.* The resize was reversed. The replacement seeds the truncation
instead: it draws from the algorithm's own seeded generator and delegates every
decision back to `pymoo`, at `pymoo`'s own default archive size of 200. That
changes which random draws occur and not what the algorithm does with them. A
second lesson was recorded from the same episode and it is general: **a test that
guards a branch must demonstrate the branch was entered.** The original
reproducibility test had run at a budget where the archive never overflowed, so it
asserted a property it could not observe and would have passed with the defect
present. The replacement counts the truncation calls and fails if the count is
zero.

### 7.8 A reference front whose density was the parametrisation's

*What was wrong.* The reference front is built by sampling the weight simplex and
pushing the sample through the map w ↦ x(w) of section 4.2. That map is rational
and nowhere an isometry, so a sample evenly spread on the simplex is **not**
evenly spread on the front: it piles up where the map contracts. IGD averages over
the **reference** points, so a region of the front carrying twice the reference
points counts twice, and a solver that covers the piled-up region well scores
better than one that covers the thin region equally well. That is a property of
the weight parametrisation and not of the solvers.

*How it was caught.* By measuring the density directly. The coefficient of
variation of the nearest-neighbour spacing of a 1000-point front was 0.74, 1.13
and 1.42 under the three φ.

*What it would have cost.* A biased IGD in every table that carries one — and the
bias does not vanish with density: the IGD of one fixed test front differs by
5.07% to 30.41% between the biased and the corrected reference at 1000 points and
still by 5.07%, 12.73% and 26.20% at 20 000. Two fronts of equal fill distance
are ranked in **opposite orders** by the two references, in 31 of 32 mirror pairs
under φ_LS and φ_CW.

*What was done.* A second sampling mode was added, with **no default**, so that a
caller must state which reference object it means: the same draw at ten times the
size, subsampled by greedy farthest-point selection in objective space. The
coefficients of variation fall to 0.12, 0.16 and 0.20. The oversampling factor of
ten is measured and not chosen: it takes at least 94% of the available reduction
at a quarter of the cost of forty. **The derivation is untouched** — every kept
point is still x(w) at a drawn weight, the kept front is a subsequence of the
oversample, and the closed-form regions of section 4.3 remain nowhere in the
computational code, living only in the tests that check the sampled points
against them, so that a passing test means two independent things agree rather
than one file agreeing with itself. The correction reaches IGD alone: the
validation gate measures a Hausdorff distance, which is a maximum, and the
dominance checks are relations, and no density enters either.

## 10. Where the project is, what remains, and the calendar

### 10.1 State

Phases a (formulation), b (ground truth) and c (solvers) are complete and tagged.
Phase d (analysis instruments) is complete in code: the decision-space metrics —
Hausdorff distance, asymmetric δ-coverage in both directions, overlap and
cross-evaluation, all computed on decision vectors — the objective-space metrics,
and the tables and figures. The last of these enforces in the artefact the
restriction that holds in the code: a table refuses to be written if a row omits
the seed count, the budget, the cardinality, the reference size, the sampling
mode, the singular-segment flag, the hypervolume reference point with the rule
that produced it, δ, the scale of the decision box, or a φ pair's status.

**No experiment has been run.**

### 10.2 What remains for part 1: three sessions

1. **Tier 0 run.** All three solvers, all three φ, all seeds, on p1 and p0, with the tables and the figures.
2. **Tier 1 run.** The same on ZDT1 and DTLZ2 across the imprecision sweep ε ∈ {0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50}, with the rank-1 size against the population size reported for every configuration.
3. **Synthesis.** How much the efficient sets differ across φ, measured in the decision space, with variance across seeds and under which imprecision levels the difference appears or vanishes; plus the solver comparison under each fixed φ with random search as the reference; plus the choice of which φ is carried into part 2, with the criterion for that choice stated explicitly, since part 1 cannot rank φ on quality.

Then part 2, the portfolio application: interval returns built from five years of
daily data on thirty assets under two constructions, optimization under the
selected φ, and an out-of-sample backtest against Markowitz, equal-weight and
risk parity. **That is where "which φ gives better results" becomes a well-posed
question**, because out-of-sample performance is a criterion external to the
transformed problem and applies equally to every φ. Inside part 1 it is not
well-posed: hypervolume computed under two different φ are volumes measured in
two different spaces, and ranking them would be a category error. Part 1
therefore measures **change**, in the decision space, which is common to every φ.

If the calendar runs short, the minimum presentable path is tier 0 only, with the
decision-space metrics and the correctness gate. That is a complete result and
not a truncated one: the solvers were validated against known answers, and the
effect of φ was measured where it is measurable.

### 10.3 What the experiments will and will not settle

**For p1 the answer is already known analytically**, including its magnitude:
§4.3 gives not only the three efficient sets in closed form but the exact overlap
of the pair that carries the signal, 10.3% of the union. So the experiments there
cannot discover what the sets are, and they cannot discover how far apart they
are either. What they add is two things the derivation cannot supply. The same
quantities computed on **recovered** sets rather than on derived ones — Hausdorff
distance, δ-coverage in both directions, overlap and cross-evaluation, with
variance across seeds — which is what the memoria can report about a method
rather than about a pair of regions, and which is where a difference could turn
out to be smaller than the seed-to-seed spread. And **how well each solver
recovers a known answer** under each order, which is already partly in hand from
the gate, §5. The analytic number of §4.3 is the yardstick those are read
against, not a substitute for them.

**For the benchmarks there is no closed form and the result is genuinely
exploratory.** No reference front exists for ZDT1 or DTLZ2 as interval problems,
so IGD is not defined there unless a reference is agreed, and the decision-space
metrics are all that can be compared across φ. What tier 1 can establish is
whether the effect measured on p1 survives at 30 and 12 variables and at 4 and 6
transformed objectives, and how it moves with the imprecision level. What it
cannot establish is whether any solver found the efficient set, because nothing
says where that set is.

Two constraints on how any of it may be read, both already established:

- **The φ comparison is made on random search output.** Only there is one sample filtered under each order, so the three fronts differ only through the order. In NSGA-II and MOPSO, φ drives the search as well as the ordering; those are reported separately, as a question about how solvers behave under each order, with the coverage deficit of §5.4 and the rank-1 saturation of §6.3 stated beside them.
- **Two of the three pairs are not free.** ND_LU and ND_CW both sit inside ND_LS, so one direction of every coverage and overlap statistic on the two φ_LS pairs is fixed before a solver runs. The headline number comes from Example 2.2 against Example 2.4.
